
Q-Learning-Based RACH Parameter Optimization for Remote AMI
Smart-Meter Deployments over NB-IoT Non-Terrestrial Networks
1. Introduction
Utility companies have spent the better part of a decade rolling out NB-IoT smart meters across cities and towns, and for the most part the technology has delivered on its promise — ten-year battery life, deep in-building penetration, and a cellular backhaul that does not need a dedicated gateway. The problem shows up at the edges of the grid. A transformer feeding forty households in a hill district, or a set of agricultural feeder meters spread across a few hundred square kilometres of farmland, often sits outside any terrestrial NB-IoT cell, and running fibre or adding a tower there rarely pencils out. This is precisely the gap that 3GPP set out to close when it brought NB-IoT into Non-Terrestrial Networks starting with Release 17: a satellite becomes the base station, and a meter that would otherwise be unreachable gets a path home.
The problem is that NB-IoT's random-access procedure — the NPRACH exchange every meter goes through before it can report a reading — was never built with a satellite link in mind. It assumes a base station a few kilometres away, not one passing overhead at roughly 7.5 kilometres a second from several hundred kilometres up. Two consequences follow directly from that mismatch. First, one-way propagation delay to a low-Earth-orbit satellite runs into the low single-digit milliseconds at closest approach and climbs toward 8 milliseconds near the horizon (for a 600 km reference orbit, worked out in Section 7) — several orders of magnitude above a terrestrial cell's sub-millisecond delay — and it changes continuously as the satellite crosses the sky, so a response window sized for a terrestrial cell can expire before a reply from the satellite is physically able to arrive. Second, the satellite's own velocity imposes a Doppler shift that reaches into the tens of kilohertz at typical NB-IoT uplink frequencies — large next to NB-IoT's 3.75 kHz preamble subcarrier spacing — and even after a UE pre-compensates using broadcast ephemeris, a residual error on the order of a few to a few hundred hertz remains, and that residual error degrades how reliably the base station can detect a preamble that was, in fact, sent correctly.
This matters for access-control tuning specifically. Access Class Barring, the Backoff Indicator, and the NPRACH configuration exist to manage contention: they decide how many devices are allowed to attempt access in a given period, and for how long a device that fails should wait before trying again. A parameter set tuned for a terrestrial cell has no way to tell a detection failure — a preamble that was sent cleanly but simply not decoded because of Doppler or a weak link at low elevation — apart from a genuine contention collision. It treats both as congestion and responds to both the same way, which is wrong in one direction or the other depending on which failure actually happened. Prior work on learning-based RACH tuning, including the Q-learning framework this project builds on, was developed and validated entirely against terrestrial traffic and a stationary channel. Whether that framework still behaves sensibly once the channel itself becomes time-varying and the observation window is bounded by a single satellite pass has not been tested, and that is the question this paper investigates.
2. Literature Review
The relevant work falls into three groups that, so far, have not been brought together.
2.1 Learning-based access-class-barring and RACH tuning on terrestrial NB-IoT
Tello-Oquendo et al. [1] provided one of the earlier rigorous treatments of ACB parameter configuration under massive M2M load in LTE-A, establishing the delay-versus-congestion trade-off that every later learning-based scheme has tried to improve on. Bui and Pham [2] used a dueling deep Q-network to balance access delay against device energy consumption, showing that removing the barring-time component from ACB helps delay at the cost of extra listening energy on the device side — a trade-off worth keeping in mind for battery-powered meters. Abera et al. [3] report a dueling double-DQN scheme reaching access success probability above 0.999 under simulated traffic. Daraseliya et al. [4], published this January, take a different route — an XGBoost classifier at the base station infers how many devices collided purely from the received SNR pattern, and uses that inference to drive reactive barring, holding preamble success near 0.3 under overload conditions where conventional ACB falls below 0.05. None of these four papers, despite being current and methodologically strong, model anything beyond a static terrestrial channel.
2.2 NB-IoT random access under satellite propagation conditions
Chougrani, Kisseleff, Martins and Chatzinotas [5] laid the groundwork here, analysing how long propagation delay and the differential delay across a satellite's footprint degrade NPRACH preamble detection and uplink synchronization, and pointing out that a TDD guard time sized for terrestrial delay becomes wastefully large under NTN conditions. A related IEEE Internet of Things Journal paper [6] proposes a unified NPRACH detector that works across both terrestrial and non-terrestrial deployments without relying on GNSS, deriving false-alarm and miss-detection probabilities analytically under high Doppler and long differential delay. A companion study [7], validated experimentally on an Open Air Interface testbed rather than purely in simulation, confirms that delay-driven access failures in NTN are not just a theoretical artefact. All of this cluster works at the physical and timing layer; none of it touches the access-control parameters themselves.
2.3 Machine learning applied to satellite random access more broadly
Outside the NB-IoT/NPRACH framework specifically, a 2024 IEEE Communications Surveys and Tutorials paper [8] surveys random-access protocols for direct-access LEO IoT and treats Doppler compensation and timing advance as recurring, unsolved themes across protocol families. A 2025 paper in Physical Communication [9] applies Q-learning with overload control to LEO satellite mMTC random access, which is the closest thing in the literature to this project's aim — except that it is built around SCMA/NOMA multiple access rather than NB-IoT's ACB-and-NPRACH framework, so the state space and action space are both different from a real Rel-17 NB-IoT deployment. A 2024 study on data-driven transmission scheduling for LEO NB-IoT [10] is worth flagging separately because it names the constraint this project's design has to respect: many LEO constellations serving IoT devices provide only a short visibility window per pass, and any adaptive scheme has to be useful within that window rather than assuming an indefinite learning horizon. A 2025 paper on LEO random access under large differential delay and Doppler shift [11] gives concrete magnitude figures — differential delay up to roughly 700 microseconds and Doppler shift up to roughly ±41 kHz — used here as a sanity check on the Doppler values the orbit model produces. Finally, a comparative study of tabular Q-learning, learning-automata Q-learning and deep Q-networks for NB-IoT resource configuration [12] is the closest terrestrial methodological precedent for the tabular-Q-learning approach used here, and its hyperparameter choices are a reasonable starting point before retuning for the satellite case.
2.4 Where this leaves the field
The terrestrial ACB/RACH-tuning literature has built genuinely good learning-based controllers, but every one of them assumes a channel that does not move. The NB-IoT-NTN literature understands the channel very well but stops at the physical layer — none of it asks whether the access-control parameters, not just the receiver algorithms, need to adapt. And the one paper that brings Q-learning to satellite random-access overload control does so for a different multiple-access scheme entirely. On the evidence gathered here, nobody has taken a Q-learning ACB / Backoff-Indicator / NPRACH controller built for terrestrial NB-IoT and tested — let alone adapted — it against the delay, Doppler and bounded-visibility conditions of an actual LEO pass. This is a search snapshot rather than an exhaustive one; a fast-moving field like this deserves one more manual Scholar check close to submission.
3. Research Gaps
Learning-based RACH controllers exist and work well, but only under a channel that never changes.
Satellite-aware NB-IoT random-access work exists and is analytically solid, but it stops at detection and synchronization — it never asks the controller layer to respond to what it knows about the channel.
No reviewed scheme separates a detection failure from a contention failure when tuning access parameters under NTN conditions, so any existing scheme, transplanted as-is to satellite operation, will misread one kind of failure as the other and respond incorrectly.
None of the reviewed NTN random-access work is framed around metering traffic or a bounded-battery meter's constraints — it is written for generic IoT devices, not for a meter reporting on a fixed schedule with a hard energy budget and no tolerance for repeated failed attempts.
4. What Needs to Be Done
The paper needs to show, with numbers rather than assertion, that a Q-learning controller which knows where the satellite is in its pass — and therefore roughly what delay and Doppler to expect — outperforms one that does not, and that the improvement survives contact with a channel model built from real orbital mechanics rather than a convenient approximation.
A channel model grounded in an actual orbit. Pull a real two-line element set for a LEO satellite in an orbit consistent with current or planned NB-IoT-NTN service, and use it — not a hand-picked constant-delay assumption — to compute elevation, range, range rate and therefore Doppler across a full pass.
An access model that distinguishes why an attempt failed. The simulator needs to log, separately, whether a RACH attempt failed because two meters picked the same preamble (collision), because the preamble was sent but not detected (a Doppler- or link-budget-driven miss), or because the response arrived after the RAR window had already closed (a delay-driven timeout). Without that separation, a claimed improvement in “access success” is not falsifiable — a reviewer will ask which failure mode actually improved.
A Q-learning agent whose state includes what the network can plausibly know. The state needs an estimate of current load, a discretised elevation or delay bin, a discretised residual-Doppler bin, and a measure of how much of the pass remains — the last one matters because a controller that needs several hundred episodes to converge is useless if a pass only lasts a few minutes. The action space stays the same three parameters used in the terrestrial work: ACB barring factor, Backoff Indicator, and NPRACH configuration (preamble repetition / coverage-enhancement group).
A convergence strategy that respects the pass-length constraint. Training the agent cold, from scratch, on every new pass is not realistic against a usable window of roughly two to seven minutes (see Section 7.1). The paper needs to test whether a Q-table trained on terrestrial or early-pass conditions and then fine-tuned, rather than learned from zero, converges within that window — and needs to show what happens when a purely terrestrial-trained table is applied to the satellite channel unmodified, since that comparison (formalised as a mandatory baseline in Section 7.3) is what proves or disproves the claim that satellite conditions genuinely require adaptation.
Baselines that make the comparison fair. At minimum: fixed ACB with a fixed Backoff Indicator (today's default), a Rel-17-style NPRACH-NTN default configuration, the terrestrial Q-table applied without modification (the critical ablation), and, if time allows, an oracle controller with perfect channel knowledge as an honest upper bound.
Metrics, reported with variance, not just a mean. Access success probability, mean and 95th-percentile access delay, the failure-cause breakdown described above, and how many episodes or passes the agent needs before its performance stabilises. Run enough independent passes (different orbital geometries, different traffic loads) to report a confidence interval, not a single simulation run dressed up as a result.
A sensitivity sweep. Vary device density, orbit altitude (to get a range of Doppler and pass-duration conditions), and residual-Doppler severity, and show where the proposed scheme's advantage grows or shrinks.
5. What the Graphs Need to Show
Access success probability versus elevation angle across a pass, for the proposed scheme against each baseline — the headline figure, expected to show a U-shape: poor near the horizon, good near zenith.
Access delay distribution (box plot or CDF, not just a bar of means) comparing the proposed scheme to the fixed-ACB and terrestrial-frozen baselines.
Failure-cause breakdown over the pass — a stacked area or bar chart showing collision, detection and timeout failures separately; this is the figure that shows the controller is responding to the right cause rather than getting lucky.
Convergence curve within a single pass — reward or success rate against episode number, with terrestrial-warm-start and cold-start variants overlaid.
Sensitivity to device density and Doppler severity — a small-multiple or heatmap view showing where the advantage holds and where it narrows.
Orbit and pass geometry itself — elevation, delay and Doppler plotted against time for the chosen pass, so a reader can sanity-check the channel conditions driving everything else.
6. How Python Should Be Used
Skyfield (SGP4 propagation from a real TLE) generates the pass geometry — elevation, range, range-rate, and from range-rate the Doppler shift at the NB-IoT carrier frequency. NumPy and SciPy handle the Monte Carlo simulation of NPRACH contention and the Q-learning update loop; tabular Q-learning needs nothing heavier, and introducing a deep-learning framework here would raise exactly the kind of on-device computational-overhead objection that does not hold up for a meter-side or even base-station-side deployment. Pandas is useful for logging per-attempt outcomes (collision / miss / timeout) in a form that is easy to aggregate into the failure-breakdown figure. Matplotlib produces the six figures above. None of this needs MATLAB, and running it in Python means a reviewer, or a student picking this project up later, can reproduce every result without a licence.
